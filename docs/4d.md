# A fourth spatial axis

The engine runs its world in 4D. `voxel_engine` with no flags puts you in
one: terrain generated from 4D Perlin noise, of which the screen shows a
3D hyperplane slice. `E` and `Q` travel along w, the scroll wheel rotates
the slice, and `--3d` gives the old engine back unchanged.

This document is in the order the work happened, because the order is the
argument. It opens with the question that decided whether any of it was
worth building - answered in a day rather than discovered in week four -
then the generator, then what a step along w costs, then the rotation
that turned out to be the whole point, and finally the edit model.

Jump to [the part that makes it look four-dimensional](#the-part-that-makes-it-look-four-dimensional)
if you only want the mechanic, or to [what is verified](#what-is-verified)
if you want to know what holds it up.

## The question

You cannot draw four dimensions on a screen. The practical approach is to
render a 3D hyperplane slice at the player's current `w`, so moving along
the fourth axis swaps which slice you see and the world appears to morph.

That only means something if the slices are related. If `w` and `w+1` are
unrelated worlds, the fourth dimension is a seed change with extra steps:
you would not be moving through anything, you would be teleporting between
random worlds, and no amount of engine work fixes that.

So: **does a step along `w` morph the world coherently?**

These are one world unit apart, which is **not** what a single frame or a
single rebuild looks like - it is roughly two and a half seconds of
holding the travel key. At that distance 88% of columns have moved and
the largest has moved 18 blocks, so w=0 and w=1 read as different places.
A single rebuild, the granularity the player actually sees, moves 35% of
columns by at most two blocks: the terrain flows.

What the three images establish is the property the whole design rests
on. The world at any `w` is a continuous function of it - there is no
frame where it jumps - and travelling far enough gets you somewhere
genuinely different rather than to a re-rolled version of the same place.
Eight units in, the ocean and island chain of w=0 have become highland
lakes, and every intermediate `w` is a world in its own right.

Regenerate with:

    cmake --build build --target slice4d
    ./build/slice4d              # seed 1337, 24 slices into docs/media/slice4d

That command reproduces the three images above byte for byte. It did not
always: `slice4d` defaulted to 0.35 world units per slice, so `w_01.png`
was actually w=0.35 and `w_08.png` was w=2.80 while the captions said one
step and eight. The default is one unit per slice now, so the file name
is the w value.

## What it cost to find out

Two things had to exist first.

**4D noise, because FastNoiseLite stops at three arguments.** It exposes
`GetNoise(x, y)` and `GetNoise(x, y, z)` and nothing else, so the noise is
the one part of a 4D terrain generator that cannot be reused and has to be
written. `src/world/noise4d.cpp` is 4D gradient (Perlin) noise: 16
hypercube corners, 32 gradients, quintic fade, quadrilinear interpolation.

**A harness that renders slices**, `src/bench/slice4d.cpp`, which is where
the two findings below came from.

## Two things the harness found

**1. The fourth axis needs its own scale after all - and both earlier
answers were right about what they measured.**

This entry has said 6, then 1, and now says 6 again. The round trip is
the useful part, because each step was a correct measurement of the wrong
quantity.

It was 6, justified by a sweep showing that at scale 1 a step along `w`
changed 4.6% of columns by at most one block - "frozen along the fourth
axis". That sweep was taken against a height amplitude of 28, and when
the amplitude was refitted to 126 nobody re-measured, so the
justification described a generator that no longer existed. Re-measured,
scale 1 moves 51.2% of columns per **world unit** of `w`. Not frozen. So
it went to 1.

Correct about the field, and wrong about the engine, because the field
scale and the player's travel speed **multiply** - and only one of them
is free.

A rebuild re-requests every chunk in the window, so how often the world
is rebuilt is set by travel speed alone, and the worker pool caps that
near three per second. Travel speed is therefore expensive and bounded
around 0.4 units/sec. `kWScale` costs nothing: it changes how different
each rebuild looks, not how many there are.

At scale 1 with a walk of 0.4 units/sec, a second of holding the key
moved 19% of columns by **one block**. Continuous, and invisible. That is
what "nothing is happening" meant when the engine was first played.
Measured per rebuild and per second of walking:

| `kWScale` | per rebuild (0.12w) | per second walking (0.4w) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 5.3%, jump 1 | 19.4%, **jump 1** |
| 2 | 11.9%, jump 1 | 38.8%, jump 2 |
| 4 | 23.7%, jump 1 | 62.0%, jump 3 |
| **6** | **35.3%, jump 2** | **73.9%, jump 5** |
| 10 | 52.6%, jump 3 | 83.5%, jump 7 |

6 is where a second of travel visibly reshapes the landscape while a
single rebuild still moves nothing by more than two blocks, so the
terrain flows rather than stepping. 10 makes each rebuild a jolt; 1 and 2
are invisible at walking pace.

The lesson is not about this constant. It is that a measurement of a
component can be exactly right and still not answer the question the
system is asking - the field's behaviour per world unit was never in
dispute, and it was never the thing that decided whether the fourth
dimension could be seen.

Continuity along `w` does not depend on any of this. That is established
by `test_adjacent_slices_are_related_not_unrelated`, and it holds at
every scale in the table.

The root cause of the stale table was duplication: `slice4d.cpp` carried
its own copy of `kWScale`, the height formula and the amplitude, and
drifted the moment the amplitude was refitted. A harness that measures a
copy of the thing measures nothing. It calls `world::TerrainGen4D` now,
so its `SLICE4D` line is the shipped behaviour by construction - and at
one world unit per slice it prints 88.1% of columns changed, max jump 18,
which is the scale-6 row of the sweep above rather than the scale-1 row
the doc used to quote.

**2. Two more constants were copied across a change of noise, and nobody
noticed until a review measured them.**

Same class as the height amplitude, in places easier to miss.

`kCaveIsoWidth` was 0.05, taken from the 3D generator - whose cave fields
are **OpenSimplex2**, not Perlin. The comment beside it called the two
"the same construction", which is true of the construction and false of
the distribution: OpenSimplex2 at this frequency has stddev 0.435 against
this noise's 0.219, so `|n| < 0.05` caught 17.8% of cells here against
7.9% there. More than twice the carving, reading as a cavier world rather
than as anything wrong.

The desert threshold `temp > 0.35` had the same problem in the other
direction, firing on 3.6% of columns against the 3D generator's 13.3% -
deserts roughly 3.7x rarer.

Both are matched by **quantile** now, not by scaling with the ratio of
standard deviations. That was the first attempt and it was not good
enough: Perlin's distribution is not Gaussian, so the stddev ratio gave
0.24 for the desert threshold where the value with the same tail mass is
0.2114. The shipped values are 0.022 and 0.21.

The observable desert fraction still differs from the 3D generator (~5%
of land against ~14%), and that part is a height distribution difference
rather than a threshold one. This generator has no lake pass, so the
amplitude is fitted wide enough that terrain reaches below sea level on
its own: 31.7% of columns sit at or below the beach band against the 3D
generator's 5.3%, leaving only 42% in the band where a desert can appear
against 77%. 13.3% of 42% is the 5% measured.

None of this was caught by a test, and one test was actively covering for
it: deleting the desert biome outright passed all 29 terrain checks,
because `sand_seen > 0` was satisfied by shoreline sand alone. Sand above
the beach band - which a shoreline can never produce - is asserted now,
bounded from both sides, and the sweep widened from radius 4 to 6 because
the temperature field's ~166-unit features meant a radius-4 window could
contain no desert at all.

**3. The 3D generator's height formula does not transfer.**

Dropping the composite noise into `kSeaLevel + n * 28 + 14`, which is what
`terrain_gen.cpp` uses, put every column between y=31 and y=46. No water
anywhere, and the whole world inside the grass/stone/snow bands. The first
render was a blob map for exactly that reason.

The cause is that this `fbm` normalizes by the amplitude sum and
FastNoiseLite's does not, so the composite here measures p1..p99 of
-0.17..0.21 rather than filling `[-1, 1]`. Refitted to the measured
distribution, `n * 126 + 8` spreads the world across roughly y=12..56 with
about a fifth of it under sea level. (126, not the 115 first used: the
noise normalizer later dropped from 0.81 to 0.75 for the reason below,
which shrank the composite and required refitting again. The amplitude is
a function of the noise's scale, so the two move together.)

Worth recording because it is the shape of what the rest of the 4D work
will look like: the algorithms port, the constants do not.

## What is verified

`tests/test_noise4d.cpp` (41 checks) and `tests/test_terrain_gen4d.cpp` (94), run by ctest. The properties, not the
outputs:

- exactly zero at every integer lattice point (an identity, not a
  tolerance), and alive between them
- pure, seed-stable, and responsive to the seed, including at seed 0 and
  seed `UINT32_MAX`
- inside `[-1, 1]`, and actually using that range
- C1 across cell boundaries: the slope does not jump
- C2 across cell boundaries: the curvature does not jump either, which is
  what separates the quintic fade from the cubic one
- isotropic: no axis moves the field faster than another, `w` included
- a fixed `w` gives a usable 3D field, and distant `w` gives a different one
- rotating the slice changes the cross section, moves columns further
  than translating the same nominal amount, and is reversible: a tilt is
  a place, not a mutation

At the engine level `--verify-4d` asserts the same claims through `World`
rather than through the generator, and reports twenty:

    VERIFY4D w=0.00 chunks=169 step_ms=80.3 changed=1 returned=1
      rapid_ok=1 held_rebuilds=114 held_travelled=0.80 held_geometry=0.69
      edit_survives_w=1 tilt_changed=1 tilt_returned=1 notch=1
      edit_survives_scroll=1 converges=1 pivot=1 ground_fixed=1
      tilt_survives_travel=1 reversible_away=1 edit_rotates=1 settled=1
      edit_ns_stable=1 bad_tris=0/0/0/0 ok

Every one of them was added because something it now covers was broken,
and the interesting half of that list is the checks that had to be
STRENGTHENED after they passed against a live defect:

- `tilt_returned` passed while rotation was irreversible, because every
  call site rotated at `player_z = 0` with `w = 0`, where the offset is
  zero and any formula returns zero to zero. `reversible_away` runs the
  same round trip 640 blocks out.
- `edit_survives_scroll` could not see the quantiser bug below, because
  it runs while `w` is still exactly `0.0f`. `edit_ns_stable` runs the
  cases that break it.
- `pivot` compares staleness COUNTS, so deleting `z_shift` from `to_4d` -
  the line that carries the pivot into the generator - passed the whole
  suite while the ground slid out from under the player. `ground_fixed`
  reads the terrain instead: the player's own row is the fixed point of a
  rotation about them, so it must not move at all.
- nothing combined the two axes. Every hash check ran at `theta = 0` and
  every rotation check at `w = 0`, so a travel that silently discarded
  the tilt passed with every field set. `tilt_survives_travel` rotates,
  travels out and back, and requires the angle intact.
- a genuine non-convergence used to HANG rather than fail - the settle
  loop ran its guard out and returned quietly, so later phases measured a
  half-built world, or CI saw an opaque timeout. `settled` reports it.
- `edit_rotates` profiled a 16x16 footprint that straddled two chunks, so
  with the freeze injected the neighbour's columns moved and it passed
  anyway. Chunk-aligned now: the injected freeze reads 0 of 256 columns
  moved against a control chunk's 252.

That is one lesson, learned four times: **a check that only ever runs
where the quantity it guards is zero is not a check.** So is a tolerance
larger than the bug - `reversible_away` allows `1e-4` of drift in `w`,
and the residue that stranded every edit in the world was `1.66e-5`,
which is why the edit namespace is no longer derived from that number.

Fifteen faults were injected to check the tests are not decoration. All
fifteen fail a named check. Three of them fail at compile time, because a
gradient table with a zero, duplicated, or over-long row shifts the field's
standard deviation by 1.6% - too small to gate on without a brittle bound,
so the property is asserted structurally instead:

    static_assert(grad4_table_is_well_formed(), ...)

The two that needed the most work are the interesting ones. Dropping `w`
from the gradient dot product survives every value, range and continuity
check, because the hash still varies with `w` and the interpolation still
runs along it - the field still responds to `w`, it just degrades to value
noise on that one axis. The isotropy test catches it: the ratio between
the fastest and slowest axis is 1.004-1.015 on correct noise and
1.58-1.60 with `w` zeroed. And swapping the quintic fade for Perlin's
original cubic passes everything except the C2 check, where the
one-sided second-derivative mismatch goes from 1.13-1.28 to 11.3-12.1.

## The generator, and what the fourth dimension costs

`src/world/terrain_gen4d.cpp` is the 3D generator's structure with `w`
threaded through every sample: domain-warped fBm for height, two
intersecting iso-surfaces for caves, altitude bands for surface material.
The frequencies port unchanged, because a frequency is about feature size
in world units and that does not depend on how many axes there are. The
amplitudes did not port, for the reason above.

29 checks in `tests/test_terrain_gen4d.cpp`, and the two that matter are
the ones that decide whether `w` is a dimension at all: **a slice must be
an ordinary usable world**, and **adjacent slices must be related**. Both
failures are silent - a `w` that does nothing gives a perfectly correct
world with no fourth dimension in it, and a `w` that does too much gives
one that teleports.

### The cost, measured

A step along `w` swaps the entire visible slice, so every chunk's mesh is
invalid. That is the central engineering problem of a 4D voxel engine, and
it does not appear to be published anywhere - games that ship 4D worlds
do not report it.

`src/bench/wcost.cpp` measures it. Radius 6, 169 chunks, single-threaded
on an Apple M4:

    full rebuild per w step: 183 ms  (gen 95 + mesh 88, meshed against
                                       neighbours as the engine does)

The interesting half is what fraction of that is avoidable. Measured at
three granularities, stable across radii 4-8 and three seeds:

| unit | changes per `w` step | if re-meshing could skip the rest |
| --- | --- | --- |
| chunk (today's mesher) | **100%** | 183 ms, 1.00x |
| section (renderer's unit) | **~22%** | 108 ms, **1.69x** |
| column (theoretical floor) | ~56% | 141 ms, 1.30x |

**Chunk-granular incremental re-meshing is worth exactly nothing**, and
that was not the expectation going in - it was one of three optimisations
this work set out to try. Every chunk in the window changes on every step,
so there is nothing to skip. The idea is dead at this scale.

Sections are where the headroom is, and column granularity - which sounds
finer and therefore better - is *worse* than sections. The reason is the
same for both, and it is the actual finding: **the fourth dimension mostly
moves the surface.** A section is a 32-block vertical slab, so the ones
deep underground are solid rock at every `w` and the ones high above the
terrain are empty at every `w`; only the few straddling the surface
change. A column spans all 256 blocks, so if any part of it moves the
whole column counts as changed, and most columns have something moving
somewhere.

That is actionable rather than a curiosity: a 4D engine should mesh at
section granularity, not chunk granularity. Today's mesher builds a
chunk-wide mesh and then buckets it into sections, so this is a real
redesign and not a tuning knob - but it is now a redesign with a number
attached.

    cmake --build build --target wcost && ./build/wcost 6

### How far the world goes

Coordinates are floats and a float carries 24 bits of mantissa, so past a
point two nearby world positions round to the same float and the noise
returns the same value for both. The terrain does not error when that
happens. It goes flat, which is the quietest failure a world advertising
no bounds could have. Distinct values among 2000 samples half a unit
apart:

| distance from origin | distinct of 2000 |
| --- | --- |
| 1e2 | 1973 |
| 1e4 | 1984 |
| 1e6 | 1945 |
| 1e7 | **595** |
| 1e8 | **1** |

So the usable range is about a million world units, 62,500 chunks from
the origin - far past anywhere a player reaches, and a written limit
rather than something discovered later as "the terrain went flat out
there". `test_the_field_keeps_its_detail_out_to_a_million_units` pins it,
and extending that test to 1e7 fails it, which is how the bound is known
to be real rather than decorative.

Never exactly 2000 even at the origin: float spacing means a few samples
of 2000 collide anywhere. An earlier version of this table read
2000/2000/2000/998/1, because the test counted `v != prev` - consecutive
differences, not distinct values. That metric would have scored a field
alternating between two values forever as perfect, and it understated the
real loss: 998 at 1e7 where the true figure is 595, so this section said
"half the detail gone" when 70% was gone.

This is not specific to the 4D work. FastNoiseLite's `GetNoise` is
templated on `FNfloat`, so the ceiling is not its default - it is
`terrain_gen.cpp`'s call sites, which pass `static_cast<float>`. The 3D
generator shares the limit for that reason rather than by inheritance.
Nothing returns NaN or infinity at any magnitude or sign - it degrades
rather than exploding, and there is a test for that too, because a NaN
would pass straight through the height clamp (NaN compares false against
both bounds) and into chunk contents.

### The range was not a bound

`sample()` is documented as returning roughly [-1, 1], and the normalizer
that makes that true was fitted to a raw peak of 1.2244 found over 20
million samples and 8 seeds.

An adversarial review swept 200 million across 200 seeds, found a raw
peak of 1.2572, and produced a normalized sample at **1.018**. The bound
was never a bound; the test simply had not met a violating sample yet -
which is exactly the "coin flip on the next sample rather than a bound"
that the constant's own comment warned against while being an instance
of it.

The normalizer is 0.75 now, not the 0.7954 that would exactly fit the
wider sweep's peak. Widening a sweep can only push the observed maximum
up, so a constant fitted to the last sweep's maximum is guaranteed to be
wrong eventually; one with ~6% of margin is not. `noise4d_range` sweeps
200M across 200 seeds rather than 20M across 8.

## What the verification does not prove

The 4D Perlin implementation was checked against an independently written
reference - corners addressed as an explicit array and collapsed
w->z->y->x, the opposite order to the implementation's x->y->z->w -
agreeing to 7.2e-07 over 1.6 million points across four seeds.

Multilinear interpolation is order-independent only if the bit-to-offset
mapping is right, so that agreement pins the corner bit assignment, the
sixteen distance vectors, which faded weight applies at which collapse
stage, and the nesting arithmetic. That is the part of hand-written 4D
Perlin most likely to be subtly wrong, and it is right.

**That is all it proves**, and the gap is worth stating because
"validated against an independent reference" sounds like more than it is.
Anything the reference shares with the implementation is invisible to it.
The shared ingredients are the gradient table, the hash, the fade
polynomial, the `& 31` mask, the normalizer and the float-to-int
conversion. Ranked by what residual risk each carries:

**The gradient table: closed, and by the `static_assert` rather than by
the reference.** There are exactly 32 vectors that are permutations of
(0, ±1, ±1, ±1) - four zero-positions times eight sign combinations. The
assert demands 32 rows, each with exactly three non-zero components in
{-1,0,1}, all pairwise distinct, and thirty-two distinct vectors meeting
that description are necessarily the complete set. The table is pinned
entirely, not sanity-checked. Only row order is free, and order does not
matter. A retyped reference table would have agreed with itself and
proved nothing.

**The global sign: was open, and nothing could see it.** Taking the
distance vector as `(corner - sample)` rather than `(sample - corner)` is
an ordinary transcription slip, and since the dot product is linear in
it, the result is exactly a negated field. Injected, **all 67 checks
across both 4D test files passed** - a negated Perlin field is still
zero-mean, isotropic, C1, C2, in range and zero at lattice points. It is
valid noise. The reference shared the convention, so the differential
check was blind too. This is the cleanest demonstration available of what
the reference does not buy.

Closed now by a known-answer test, deliberately a golden test in a file
that otherwise pins relationships: a sign convention has no relational
anchor, exactly like the CRC polynomial `test_crc_known_answer` pins in
`test_world.cpp`. Re-injecting the reversal now fails with
`expected 0.328125, got -0.328125`.

**The normalizer: open by construction**, and this is the one that
mattered. The reference was handed the same constant, so it said nothing
about calibration - and calibration is precisely what turned out to be
broken. It took an independent 200M-sample sweep to find. The oracle
covers algebra, not constants.

**Gradient selection uniformity: verified once, not guarded.** A sweep of
164,000,000 lattice corners across four seeds found the 32 bins spread by
0.194%, chi-square 28.1 on 31 degrees of freedom against a p=0.001
critical value of 61.1. Uniform. But that was a one-off measurement, and
nothing in the suite would notice if it later stopped being true: the
index is not observable through the public surface, and a histogram of
output values is a poor proxy because the field is bell-shaped. An
attempt at one was removed rather than tuned, since fitting the buckets
to the field's own distribution would have made it pass for a reason
unrelated to its claim. Cross-cell correlation and lattice-scale
periodicity are uncovered for the same reason.

## The part that makes it look four-dimensional

Everything above is about TRANSLATING along w: the slice is the hyperplane
`w = constant`, and stepping moves it. That was the whole engine for
several rounds, and it was not enough. Walking through it, the complaint
was exact:

> if its 4D why is it like all cubic then

It was right, and the reason is geometric rather than a matter of tuning.
A `w = constant` slice is axis-aligned to the 4D lattice, so every block
meets the cut square-on and every block is a whole cube. Travelling swaps
one perfectly ordinary 3D world for another perfectly ordinary 3D world.
Nothing is ever seen in cross section, so nothing ever looks like a
four-dimensional object being cut.

[4D Miner](https://4d-miner.fandom.com/wiki/4D_Miner) is the game that
gets this right, and its wiki says how: the player rotates their 3D slice
with the scroll wheel, and "the blocks will seem to change their shape. In
reality, the game's 4D blocks never change their shape, but different
slices of the 4D world can contain unique cross sections of 4D blocks."

So the slice is an orientation as well as an offset. `TerrainGen4D::Slice`
carries `{w, theta}`, and one function rotates in the (z, w) plane:

    z4 = z cos(theta) - (kWScale * w) sin(theta)
    w4 = z sin(theta) + (kWScale * w) cos(theta)

The `kWScale` has to be inside the rotation, and that is not a detail. It
was applied afterwards at first, which composes to `[[c, -s], [6s, 6c]]`:
a determinant of 6, with singular values 1 and 6 instead of 1 and 1. The
rotation was proper, but the space it turned was six times finer along w
than along z, so a tilt progressively squashed the world along z - the
ratio of mean slope along z to along x went 1.00 at theta=0 to 4.79 at
pi/2, where the terrain corrugates into ridges. It also broke the
no-cliffs invariant the suite asserts, which had only ever been checked
at theta=0. Scaling first makes it a true rotation of an isotropic space,
and leaves theta=0 untouched.

At `theta = 0` this is the old axis-aligned hyperplane, unchanged, which
is why every figure above still reproduces. At `theta = pi/2` the slice's
own z axis **is** the fourth dimension, so walking forward walks through w.
Everything between is a genuine mixture. The whole convention lives in
`to_4d`, so nothing else has to know it.

Rotation is far stronger than translation per unit, and the geometry says
why: a tilted hyperplane diverges from the original linearly with
distance, so the far edge of the view is displaced much more than the
near. Measured against a flat slice over a 192-block square, by
`test_terrain_gen4d.cpp`:

| tilt | columns changed | largest move |
|---|---|---|
| 0.002 rad | 4.8% | 1 block |
| 0.010 rad | 22.9% | 2 |
| 0.030 rad | 50.6% | 5 |
| 0.050 rad | 62.3% | 7 |
| pi/2 | 95.3% | 34 |

against 33.8% and 2 blocks for a whole rebuild threshold of w, and 92.1%
and 15 for translating two whole units.

The comparison of totals is the weaker half of the story, though, and an
earlier version of this table leaned on it too hard. What actually
distinguishes a rotation is that its displacement is proportional to
distance from the axis: at 0.03 rad the band 64-128 blocks out changes
more than 1.5x as many columns as the band under the player's feet, and
moves them further, while a translation is flat across the same two
bands. That is structural. No choice of w reproduces it, and it is what
the test pins - a totals comparison would be fragile, and was: at 0.01
rad a rotation's largest move is 2 blocks, exactly what a
rebuild-threshold translation gives.

The scroll step is 0.003 rad, about a fifth of a degree. The first choice
was 0.05, which moves 62% of the visible columns per notch and reads as
the scene being replaced rather than reshaped.

### Staleness is a distance, not a weighted sum

A chunk is stale when its terrain has moved far enough in the noise
field, and "far enough" is one number - a distance - for both motions.

It was `|dw| + |dtheta| * 32` at first, which had to invent a constant to
add an angle to a length. 32 was chosen to make the first scroll notch
clear the threshold rather than to describe anything, and it was wrong in
both directions at once: a chunk sitting on the rotation axis barely
moves under a tilt and was marked stale anyway, while a distant one moves
far more than 32 implies. One notch triggered a full 625-chunk rebuild to
buy a change in which no column anywhere moved more than one block.

Two things had to be true before a displacement could replace it, and
both were separate defects:

- The noise space had to be **isotropic**. While `kWScale` was applied
  after the rotation, a given distance along w meant six times more change
  than the same distance along z, so no single threshold could serve both.
- The rotation had to turn about the **player**, or the displacement of
  everything around a distant player would be dominated by how far they
  had walked rather than by how far they had turned.

What that bought, radius 12, chunks left stale when the motion stopped:

| motion | before | after |
|---|---|---|
| scroll 1 notch/s | 409 / 625 | **235** |
| scroll 15 notch/s | 625 / 625 | 581 |
| walking, for comparison | 577 / 625 | 577 |

GLFW delivers scroll by callback, so `Input` grew an accumulator drained
once per frame, clamped at eight notches: a kinetic trackpad flick
arrives as a stream of large deltas and could otherwise apply a quarter
turn in a single frame.

`--slice-tilt R` pins it for captures, the same way `--slice-w` pins the
offset.

## Building in four dimensions

An edit is a **replay list**, not a snapshot. `set_block` records the
voxel it changed under (chunk, integer w); when a chunk needs rebuilding
the terrain is generated for whatever slice is current and the edits are
replayed over it.

That is a correction, and the version it replaced had a visible symptom.
The stash used to hold the whole chunk and hand it back verbatim, which
preserved the edit and froze everything around it - so one placed block
pinned its entire 16x256x16 chunk against every further turn of the
wheel while the world rotated around it. Measured against an unedited
control chunk at the same distance from the pivot, sixty notches: the
edited chunk moved 0 of its 256 columns, the control moved 252.

Whole-chunk stashing survives for exactly the case that needs it - a
chunk the generator cannot reproduce, meaning one saved with edits
already baked in, or any chunk loaded under a different seed.

Two decisions in the key are worth stating, because both were nearly made
by accident:

**The key is an integer w, and it comes from travel.** It used to be
`floor(slice_w_)`, and rotation rewrites `slice_w_` every notch: a round
trip returns it to a residue rather than to zero, and `floor` is
one-sided there, so `-1e-16` read as slice -1 while every resident chunk
still recorded slice 0. Every edit in the world was stranded under a key
the player would never consult again. Seven notches out and back, fifty
blocks from spawn, was enough. It reads `travel_w_` now, which only
travel changes - rotating about the player does not move the player, so
it must not move their edit namespace either.

**The key carries no orientation, deliberately.** An edit made on a flat
slice is restored on a tilted one. The player built it, and turning their
view of the fourth dimension should not delete their house; keying on
theta would make anything built vanish the moment the wheel moved, which
is the opposite failure and a worse one. The cost is that two hyperplanes
sharing an integer w share a bucket, so a structure built flat reappears
in terrain a tilted cut has rearranged around it. That is the same trade
the w key already makes at coarser grain.

## What this does not do yet

The engine IS four-dimensional now: `voxel_engine` with no flags puts you
in a 4D world, `E` and `Q` travel along w, the scroll wheel rotates the
slice, and `--3d` gives the old engine back. What remains:

**Measurement paths stay 3D.** `--bench`, `--validate`, the captures and
the verify modes all use the 3D generator unless `--4d` is passed. A 4D
world at w=0 is not the world the 3D generator makes - different
amplitude, no lake pass, no trees - so defaulting them to it would move
every published figure: `gpu_mesh_mb`, the greedy ratio the CI gate pins,
and the byte-identical `BENCH_SUMMARY` the invariance check compares.
Those numbers describe the 3D engine and have to keep doing so.

**Sprinting still costs the pool the same as walking**, which is the
point of deriving the rebuild threshold from speed - but it means
sprinting takes 0.36-unit steps, and each rebuild then moves 70% of
columns rather than 35%. Bigger jumps, same cadence. Whether that reads
as speed or as coarseness is a judgement the numbers cannot settle.

**Section-granular re-meshing is unbuilt**, and it is the one measured
win left on the table: the cost model says 1.56x at radius 6 and 1.58x at
radius 8 (it was 1.70x before the generator changed, so it is worth
re-running rather than quoting), and the renderer
already culls at that unit. Today's mesher builds a chunk-wide mesh and
buckets it afterwards, so it is a real redesign rather than a knob.
